An Early Nor'easter

An early-season nor’easter brought heavy rain and strong winds to the East Coast over the weekend. At least one person was killed by a falling tree, and around 180,000 people lost power at the storm’s peak (videos of the aftermath).

Multiple New England states recorded wind gusts exceeding 60 mph and rainfall from two to six inches (what causes a nor’easter?). Coastal floods overtook roads and buildings along the Jersey Shore and Long Island. Warnings for river flooding in New Jersey and Massachusetts remained in effect yesterday. On Nantucket, nor’easter-driven waves washed remains from a shipwreck ashore. Experts believe the wooden fragments were part of a schooner that sailed in 1884.

Separately, Hurricane Nolo threatened Hawaii with gusty winds, rain, and flooding as it moved away from the islands yesterday. Its proximity prompted Hawaii’s unprecedented third hurricane watch in a single year, fueled by an exceptionally strong El Niño.

Science Museum Swan Song

The Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia closed its museum to visitors yesterday, concluding an almost 200-year run. Opened in 1828, it was the oldest natural history museum in the Western Hemisphere, containing over 19 million scientific specimens.

As with 55% of US museums, the academy struggled to return to prepandemic attendance levels, sliding from 123,550 visitors in 2019 to below 42,000 in 2021. It has been a nonprofit subsidiary of Drexel University since 2011, but the private institution said that the operational costs had become unsustainable. The university faces its own financial challenges, including a roughly 20% drop in enrollment over the last decade. Hundreds of people gathered to protest the museum’s closure, and Philadelphia City Council members scheduled a special hearing with museum leadership for Oct. 7.

The museum featured a life-size Tyrannosaurus rex cast, a live butterfly garden, and over 200 plants collected by Meriwether Lewis and William Clark. Take a virtual walk-through.

Missouri Map Mayhem

The Supreme Court rejected an effort to reinstate a reworked congressional map in Missouri late Friday, suspending a federal appeals court ruling permitting its use in the upcoming midterms. The decision—the third in recent weeks ruling in favor of a 2022 map—means some voters will cast ballots for candidates different from those in August primaries.

One of the more chaotic examples of recent midcycle redistricting efforts, the state’s legislature passed a new map favoring Republicans last September. A voter petition to use the 2022 map was ultimately found valid (see full timeline), but only after the primaries, forcing election officials to scramble to rewrite ballots. The current (from 2022) map gives the GOP a 6-2 seat advantage; the rejected map aimed for a 7-1 split.
