One Big Headline

The Stress Switch

Experiencing severe stress during childhood can cause lasting damage to brain cells, making individuals more sensitive to stress later in life. The discovery, published yesterday, may reveal a new biological target for mental health treatments.

The study focused on the chemical messenger dopamine, which helps process rewards and adversity (watch 101). Researchers found that mice raised in stressful conditions had higher levels of the enzyme SETD7 than mice raised under typical conditions. SETD7 can make dopamine-producing brain cells more responsive to environmental stimuli, leading to a lower stress tolerance. The mice with elevated SETD7 levels displayed more anxious behaviors throughout their lives. However, when researchers blocked the enzyme, these mice became less sensitive to stress.

Quick Hits

Todd Blanche secures support for attorney general confirmation.

Sen. Bill Cassidy (R, LA) said yesterday he would support President Donald Trump's pick to lead the Justice Department, clearing the way for Blanche's confirmation. The Senate aims to hold a final vote in the coming days. The Senate also confirmed 74 other Trump nominees, including former Navy SEAL Cameron Hamilton as the director of the Federal Emergency Management Agency. (More)

Separately, a federal appeals court ruled Trump must get approval from Congress to continue his $400M White House ballroom renovation. Trump said he plans to bring the decision to the Supreme Court.

Puerto Rico yesterday began rationing water amid severe drought.

More than 180,000 customer accounts in the capital city of San Juan and nearby areas will have running water cut off for 48 hours at a time. July was San Juan's driest month on record since 1899. Experts predict the drought will persist until at least September, and officials warn the rationing periods could be extended to 72 hours. The water shortages come after San Juan’s mayor sued the island’s Water and Sewer Authority in May over aging infrastructure. (More)

US employers unexpectedly shed 23,000 jobs in July.

The federal jobs report released yesterday suggests the US labor market is weaker than previously believed. Forecasters had expected payrolls to increase by 83,000 jobs last month. Meanwhile, the unemployment rate ticked down from 4.2% to 4.1%, largely because fewer people participated in the labor force. The share of those working or looking for work fell to its lowest level since February 2021. (More)

Thai student kills grandparents before deadly school shooting.

At least seven people were killed and dozens were injured in a shooting at a high school north of Bangkok yesterday. Police identified the shooter as a 14-year-old boy, who was among the deceased. The boy allegedly killed his grandparents, with whom he lived, before carrying out the school shooting and taking his own life. He is believed to have used his grandfather's registered gun in the attacks. (More)

Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan sign defense agreement.

The deal signed yesterday between the three Sunni Muslim-majority states stipulates that an attack on one will be treated as an attack on all three. Iranian officials warned it would not protect Saudi Arabia, whose critical infrastructure has come under fire as part of the war in Iran. Pakistan has an estimated 170 nuclear warheads, and Turkey has NATO's second-largest army. (More)

First poop transplants show promise as a treatment for food allergies.

Six people with severe peanut allergies can tolerate small doses of the nut after receiving gut bacteria from stool samples of nonallergic donors. The results suggest lower levels of certain gut bacteria cause food allergies. However, nine other trial participants did not respond to the treatment. The authors are planning a larger trial to further explore the approach. (More)

Humankind(ness)

Today, we're sharing a story from reader Bob H. in Anchorage, Alaska.

"While traveling in Europe a few months ago, language was sometimes a barrier. On the back of my cellphone, I carried cards I had made up with common phrases used while traveling, starting with 'Hello' in the local language. Each card's word also had the phonetic spelling, so I could attempt to say it. Using these, and even showing them to the locals, changed our interaction completely. Some looked at the cards and even helped me to pronounce them in their language. It was a fun way to communicate, and people became more friendly. A simple, courteous kindness was exchanged between us that formed a lasting memory."
