Supreme Court Term Begins

The newest Supreme Court term kicked off yesterday with a case on whether energy companies can be held liable for damage related to climate change.

Boulder sued oil companies Exxon and Suncor in 2018 over allegations that their fossil fuels worsened local heat waves and wildfires. (An analysis by the left-leaning Colorado Fiscal Institute estimates climate change will cost the state $37B over 25 years.) The companies and their supporters, including the Trump administration, seek to block the suit, arguing it would allow state law to supersede federal law regulating the environment. If allowed to move forward, the case would clear the path for dozens of similar lawsuits.

Later this term, the court will review challenges to state laws banning AR-15s that allegedly violate the Second Amendment, the Trump administration's authority to detain migrants without bond hearings and deport them to third countries, and states' ability to require proof of citizenship to vote. Cornell Campus Protests

Student activists shattered a glass window and sprayed graffiti on Cornell University's main administrative building yesterday, kicking off protests over the Ivy League school's handling of an alleged sexual assault at a fraternity.

A former Cornell student filed a lawsuit against the university, seven Chi Phi fraternity members, and others last month, claiming the school failed to protect her after she was drugged and gang-raped in 2024. The woman, identified in court documents as Jane Doe, had reported the alleged assault to campus officials, prompting the university to ban Chi Phi and impose sanctions on the fraternity members, ranging from expulsion to essay writing. The district attorney, however, did not pursue criminal charges, citing insufficient evidence of nonconsensual sexual activity. It has since emerged that Cornell omitted Jane Doe's assertion that she was raped from a report given to prosecutors.

More than 35% of female undergraduates surveyed at Cornell last year reported experiencing nonconsensual sexual contact, up from 23% in 2023.

Book Ban Boom

Public schools across the US implemented nearly 12,000 book bans over the past year, a new study revealed. The bans encompassed 7,370 individual works, marking the highest total since nonprofit organization PEN America began tracking bans in 2021.

More than a dozen states have enacted sweeping laws to censor and remove books flagged as explicit or profane since 2021. Books addressing race, sexuality, and gender identity remain among the most commonly removed materials, according to PEN’s analysis. See the most banned titles here. Texas and Florida’s combined tally accounted for around 95% of all recorded bans nationwide (scroll for map of bans by state). Supporters say parents have a right to control their children’s educational materials, while opponents contend that bans violate the First Amendment.
