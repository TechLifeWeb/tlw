Hurricane Isaias

Hurricane Isaias is expected to make landfall along the US Gulf Coast late tonight or early tomorrow as a Category 2 or higher storm. See updated forecast cone.

The storm, packing sustained winds of 100 mph as of this writing, is expected to strike between Mississippi and the Florida Panhandle, bringing storm surges of up to 7 feet, heavy rainfall, and damaging winds (why the storm’s so-called “dirty side” faces the greatest surge risk). Officials have issued evacuation orders, closed schools, and declared states of emergency in Florida and Alabama. Heavy rainfall is expected to spread inland across the eastern US this weekend. Isaias is the first Atlantic hurricane of the 2026 season, forming more than four months after the season began. Its late arrival is attributed partly to El Niño, a weather pattern that increases wind shear and disrupts hurricane formation.

The storm formed just one day shy of tying a 121-year-old record for the latest first Atlantic hurricane, set Oct. 8, 1905.

Protests Sweep Spain

Thousands of people marched through Madrid yesterday in memory of an 87-year-old tenant who died the day before. Protesters had rallied around her as a symbol of the country’s housing affordability crisis.

María del Carmen Abasca had lived in her apartment since 1956 and inherited the rent-controlled lease from her mother in 2005. She paid around $570 per month until a real estate company purchased the property in 2018 and sought to increase her rent by 230%. Abasca could not cover the new rate, and the company made four attempts to evict her. On Sept. 23, police broke down Abasca’s door, removed her on a stretcher, and took her to a hospital (watch her response).

The eviction resonated with Spaniards who now spend 50% of their salaries on rent as nationwide rates have nearly doubled over the last decade. Lawmakers rejected emergency renter protection proposals last week, prompting Spain's socialist prime minister to call a Nov. 29 snap election.

Slow and Steady Aging

Scientists identified unique genetic traits that may explain how Jonathan, a roughly 194-year-old giant tortoise and the world’s oldest land animal, has lived so long. The study results, published this week, could offer insights into human longevity.

Jonathan is believed to have hatched in 1832, the same year author Lewis Carroll and architect Gustave Eiffel were born. The 400-pound tortoise lives on a remote island off Africa’s west coast, where researchers traveled to swab his saliva. The sample revealed 287 genetic variants unique to Jonathan, including some linked to cancer suppression and inflammation reduction. Researchers also found that his molecular "on-off" switches regulating DNA repair, energy production, and other processes were resistant to wear and tear, resembling those of much younger tortoises. Changes in these switches—called the epigenome—are one of the nine hallmarks of aging (w/video).

Experts say Jonathan's longevity is likely due in part to his vegetable-heavy diet, favorable tropical climate, and attentive veterinary care. Watch him get a bath here.
