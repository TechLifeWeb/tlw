Itchy Little Fly

Tiny but lethal, mosquitoes are the world's deadliest animal, killing about 760,000 people annually. Though there are more than 3,500 species of mosquitoes and only a very small fraction of them bite humans, the diseases that skeeters carry can be very deadly. Malaria, dengue, West Nile virus, and more sicken and kill humans and other animals, too. Only females bite humans, and they suck our blood because they need blood proteins to sustain their eggs.

> Mosquitoes top the ranking of the world's deadliest animals. (More, w/chart)
> The mosquito's role in the transmission of malaria was confirmed in 1897. (More)

The bugs have been spotted on every continent but Antarctica. Their range is expanding with climate change, and mosquitoes were most recently documented in 2025 in Iceland, the last mosquito-free country until that point. Even as mosquitoes spread to new locales and the bugs develop resistance to insecticides, scientific advances are creating new mosquito control options.

> Genetic analysis suggests Anopheles mosquitoes fed on humans some 1.8 million years ago. (More)

> Anopheles mosquitoes are developing resistance to insecticides. (More)

> Iceland, the last mosquito-free country, detected them in 2025. (More, w/video)

Gene-editing technology could now theoretically render female mosquitoes infertile to wipe out disease-carrying species, though concerns remain about effectively limiting this engineered trait to the target species. Infecting mosquitoes with the Wolbachia bacterium is another option under consideration, since the naturally occurring microbe blocks transmission of various diseases.

> See how gene-editing technology could theoretically make female mosquitoes infertile. (More, w/video)

> The insect drinks a maximum of 0.01 milliliters of blood in a single meal. (More, w/video)

> Using mosquitoes to vaccinate bats could curb the spread of deadly diseases. (More)

> Mosquitoes even have olfactory receptors on their sperm. (More, w/video)

> Some mosquitoes are key pollinators—and eat disease-carrying mosquitoes. (More)
