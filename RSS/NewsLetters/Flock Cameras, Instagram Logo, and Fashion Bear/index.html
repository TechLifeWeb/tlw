Flock Changes Course

Flock Safety announced new privacy restrictions yesterday for its automated license plate readers following scrutiny over privacy and police misuse. The company will require automated audits and case codes for searches and reduce its default data retention period from 30 days to seven.

The changes follow reports of officers using the AI-powered system for personal surveillance, including stalking romantic partners. An investigation (free w/email) identified at least 50 officers accused of misusing plate-reader systems, 46 involving Flock. The company operates roughly 120,000 cameras across 49 states, capturing about 20 billion plate scans monthly. Flock says 90% of searches occur within a week, though a new “Evidence Mode” will let agencies preserve specific data longer for active investigations.

Flock says its technology was involved in more than 1 million investigations and helped locate more than 10,000 missing people last year. The network extends beyond law enforcement, with cameras operated by businesses, schools, homeowner associations, and other private groups. Are Flock cameras in your neighborhood?

West Bank Siege

Israeli troops yesterday said they dismantled two illegal outposts erected by Israeli settlers who have trapped three Palestinian families in their homes since Sunday.

Dozens of settlers surrounded three homes near Qusra, a West Bank village of about 5,000 people (watch footage). The settlers cut off water and power and blocked an access road, reportedly forcing residents to ration food. At least one home is owned by an American, prompting the US to issue a rebuke. The Israeli military said it deployed an additional infantry battalion to Qusra this week as it responds to an uptick in settler attacks; the United Nations reported an average of 190 attacks per month during the first four months of 2026, putting the West Bank on track to surpass the 1,835 attacks recorded last year.

The standoff comes after a clash near Qusra last month that killed two Israelis and four Palestinians. Two mosques were subsequently set on fire. (w/video)

Instagram Logo Redesign

Instagram has updated its wordmark—the text version of its logo—for the first time in a decade, the company revealed yesterday. See the redesign here (scroll to bottom for “before” image).

The latest, wider typeface combines sans serif and cursive lettering, unlike the 2010 wordmark, which was exclusively in cursive. Critics have characterized the redesign as an example of “blanding,” a move toward more muted colors and simplified logos. It also follows the trend of cursive logos falling out of favor, with Johnson & Johnson and Eddie Bauer turning to sans serif fonts in recent years (see before-and-after).

While rebrands can keep a company looking modern, they also risk alienating loyal customers; a study of sports team logo redesigns found color changes reduced fan loyalty more than shape redesigns. See how 30 famous brand logos, including Starbucks and Amazon, have evolved over the years.

Car insurance is one of those things that’s not necessarily on everyone’s mind too often. The problem with setting it and forgetting it, though? Some insurers take advantage by quietly raising premiums over time for long-term customers—meaning you could be paying more just for being loyal.

Plus, after years of inflation-driven spikes, car insurance premiums now average over $2,200–$2,500 annually, but that’s still more than double what drivers paid just a few years ago. The good news: Car insurance prices tumbled 6% in 2025. You don’t have to settle for a higher bill. Some ways to save include shopping around regularly, bundling your auto insurance with home or renters coverage, and focusing on safe driving.
