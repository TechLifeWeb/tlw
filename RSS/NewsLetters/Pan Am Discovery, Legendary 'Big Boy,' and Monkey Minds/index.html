Pan Am Found

The wreckage of Pan Am's Clipper Endeavor has been found off Puerto Rico, 74 years after the flight crashed into the Atlantic Ocean.

The Douglas DC-4, roughly the size of a Boeing 737, took off from San Juan on April 11, 1952, bound for New York, but shortly after departure, the plane suffered engine failure and ditched at sea. Fifty-two of the 69 people aboard died, while 17 survived the crash—two of whom are still living today. The accident prompted sweeping aviation safety changes, including the now-standard preflight safety briefing, after many passengers refused the pilot's orders to evacuate and remained strapped into their seats.

An underwater search team located the plane roughly 2,000 feet below the surface, identifying it by the still-visible Pan Am logo and the aircraft's name painted on the largely intact wreckage. War Chest Cost

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Gen. Dan Caine testified before a key Senate committee yesterday, advocating for the Trump administration’s request of $1.5T, including $67.1B in immediate funding to help cover the costs of the Iran war. Hegseth estimated the war has cost $37.5B to date. See takeaways here.

The request covers weapons, classified programs, fuel, operations, cybersecurity, and drones, among other costs. A shortfall has already reportedly forced the Pentagon to limit training and maintenance efforts; parts of the Navy and Air Force budgets are expected to run out of funds by August. The House plans to vote later this week to authorize $73B in defense spending before breaking for a weekslong recess; Democratic opposition may block the bill’s advance.

The testimony comes as President Donald Trump said the US will soon strike Iran’s Pickaxe Mountain, where he suggested Iran may have moved its centrifuges (see how nuclear enrichment works, via YouTube).

Big Boy Locomotive

The world’s largest operating steam locomotive, Big Boy 4014, arrived in Kansas City yesterday as part of a monthslong coast-to-coast tour honoring the United States’ 250th anniversary. Watch the gigantic steam engine in action here.

Big Boy 4014 was one of 25 large locomotives built for Union Pacific in the 1940s. The 1.2-million-pound machine hauled heavy freight through mountain passes during World War II. Retired in 1961 as steam engines were replaced by cheaper diesel-electric trains, Big Boy 4014 spent over five decades in a museum before Union Pacific reacquired it and converted it from coal to fuel oil. Though still too inefficient for regular service—using around 250 gallons of water and 15 gallons of fuel per mile—this summer's tour has drawn sentimental crowds (w/photos).

The locomotive began its journey at the groundbreaking site of the transcontinental railroad in Sacramento, traveled east of the Mississippi, and stopped in Philadelphia for July Fourth. It is now heading back west; track it here.
