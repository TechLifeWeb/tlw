The Space Arms Race

The US has deployed weapons in orbit, officials revealed Monday, marking the first public acknowledgment of US space-based weapons. Officials offered few details, describing them as "on-orbit space control weapons" designed to protect against hostile action.

The disclosure comes amid growing space-military competition, with the US saying that China and Russia are developing capabilities that could threaten US satellites. In 2025, the US announced plans to develop Golden Dome, a missile defense network that would include space-based interceptors to destroy missiles in flight. One Congressional Budget Office model estimated a system could require 7,800 orbital interceptors and cost $1.2T over 20 years. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty bans nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit, but does not ban conventional weapons. Meanwhile, a Pentagon watchdog found the Iran war has strained US interceptor inventories, with operations costing over $42B (see real-time cost tracker).

Treasury Yields Soar

The 10-year and 30-year Treasury yields yesterday climbed to their highest levels since 2007, the year before the global financial crisis. Investors now see a more than 92% chance the Federal Reserve will raise interest rates by 0.25% today.

Treasury yields are the annual returns the federal government pays to investors who buy bonds (get 101). The 10-year yield hit 5.04%, and the 30-year hit 5.36% yesterday, in part due to inflation fears as the Iran war sends oil prices above $106 per barrel. The 10-year yield is often used as a benchmark for borrowing costs, so its rise may drive up mortgage rates. Higher-yielding bonds could also spur investors to shift money from stocks to bonds, pushing the stock market down. US stock markets closed down on the news (S&P 500 -0.5%, Dow -0.6%, Nasdaq -0.8%).

Separately, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent defended President Donald Trump's economic record before Congress, including a $5.2B bond buyback last week aimed at driving yields down. See takeaways here.

Kennedy Center Closes

The Kennedy Center’s board, led by President Donald Trump, voted yesterday to close the landmark DC venue, citing urgent financial and structural issues. The board’s decision came soon after a federal judge ruled Trump’s name could not be readded to the building.

The board cited a partial ceiling collapse in the venue’s main lobby on Sept. 4 after an evening of heavy rain in its decision (scroll for video). However, the board said it will not begin a planned two-year, $257M renovation project funded by Congress until Trump’s name can be inscribed, an effort to honor his plan to raise an additional $100M in funding. A federal judge has ruled such inscriptions illegal, citing federal law that no memorials or plaques be added to the Kennedy Center without congressional approval.

Trump is the first current or former US president to serve as chair of the Kennedy Center, which has historically had a bipartisan governance structure. See previous write-up for more on the center’s history.
