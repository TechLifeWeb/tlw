Iran in New York

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told the UN General Assembly yesterday Tehran would not surrender its nuclear program under US pressure. His address was the first before the assembly by the president of a country at war with the US.

The speech followed the US and Tehran's first talks since June, which came after President Donald Trump threatened to "annihilate" Iran without a deal. Tehran is reviewing a US response to its peace proposal, and Pezeshkian characterized his speech as a response to Trump. He opened with photos of children killed in a US airstrike (watch here—warning: sensitive); the UN last week said the February attack may constitute a war crime. He also insisted Tehran's nuclear program is for civilian purposes, though a UN watchdog reported Iran to the Security Council this month over alleged noncompliance.

Meanwhile, a cargo ship was struck by an unidentified projectile in the Strait of Hormuz, killing at least one person and forcing 27 others to evacuate.

Cancer Detection Test

A Food and Drug Administration advisory panel voted yesterday to endorse a blood test designed to detect up to 50 forms of cancer in people aged 50 or older. The decision suggests full FDA approval may be forthcoming, opening the door to Medicare and private insurance coverage.

The DNA-based Galleri test uses a single blood draw to assess if a user may have tumors caused by cancers missed by current screenings, like ovarian and thyroid cancers. FDA panelists noted that one-third to one-half of the test’s positive results may be false but concluded its benefits outweighed the risks. There is no large-scale randomized data finding Galleri reduces cancer mortality. Biotech company Grail states the test should be used to complement existing screenings, not replace them. It retails for $949 online.

Cancer is the second-leading cause of death in the US behind heart disease. See a breakdown of 2026 case numbers and demographics here.

The Heart of Ancient Paris

Archaeologists have unearthed what may be a section of the walls surrounding ancient Paris, France’s culture ministry said yesterday. The finding is the strongest signal yet in search of Lutetia, the Latin name for Paris referenced in Julius Caesar’s “Gallic Wars.” Watch an auto-dubbed interview recorded at the dig.

Researchers discovered a roughly 70-foot-wide, stone-and-wood wall on the Île de la Cité, an island on the Seine River also hosting the Notre-Dame cathedral (scroll for bird's eye view). The wall was buried beneath Paris’ oldest working hospital, the Hôtel-Dieu, and discovered alongside a Gallic spearhead. Carbon analysis suggests it is about over 2,000 years old, overlapping with the eight-year Gallic wars chronicled by Caesar; burnt sections also align with Caesar’s descriptions, whereby the Romans won decisively but Gauls torched the city to avoid capture.
