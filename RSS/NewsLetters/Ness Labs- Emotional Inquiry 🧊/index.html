Hi friends,

A couple of days ago, I was having coffee with a good friend who was telling me about some difficult feedback she had received at work on a project, how much it had affected her emotionally, and how she wished she could be more “chill” about it.

I told her I didn’t think she should try to be chill, as there was actually so much she could learn from that emotional response if she approached it like a scientist: with curiosity rather than self-blame.

This week, I want to explore three simple steps you can use to practice emotional inquiry whenever you notice yourself having a strong reaction. I hope you find them helpful.

🧊 Emotional Inquiry #

When I had just started at Google, I raised my hand to take on a research project that was particularly scary to me, as it would be used by our partners team to make real-world decisions. The question was: why weren’t brands taking YouTube seriously, and what might convince them of the value of online video?

I know this feels obvious today, but at the time TV was still the gold standard for reaching mass audiences, and the idea of a “content creator” had barely entered the mainstream.

So, on top of my other projects, I spent hours reading about digital advertising, consumer psychology, and media strategy. I really wanted to get it right.

When I finally presented my findings, a colleague pointed out a few weaknesses in my methodology. I became so emotional that I left the call and pretended I was having internet problems. Then, in my head, that feedback quickly ballooned into doubts about whether I was cut out for a job at Google at all.

It might not feel so salient, but most of us have experienced a version of this. A colleague gives you a piece of feedback which you know isn’t particularly harsh, but you keep replaying it for the rest of the day, or the week.

That’s because we often assume that our immediate emotional responses are a direct reflection of what happened. But the event is only part of the story. Studies suggest that what also matters is what that event means to us.

Organizational psychologist Marcia Reynolds has written about the psychological needs that can underlie emotional triggers. Constructive feedback might feel threatening if being seen as competent matters deeply to you. An unexpected change might unsettle you if you value predictability. Being left out might threaten your sense of inclusion. A dismissive comment might feel especially charged when respect is especially important to you.

That doesn’t mean every emotion is revealing some profound hidden truth, but strong reactions can be useful clues.

So instead of asking, “Why am I so upset about this?”, try asking, “What feels threatened here? This shift will help you separate the trigger from the need. For instance:

Emotional inquiry examples

Once you know what the emotion may be pointing toward, you have more options. You can decide whether the need is genuinely being threatened, whether your interpretation is accurate, and what (if anything) you want to do about it.

Here’s a simple way to practice emotional inquiry:

Name what you’re feeling. Get more specific than “bad” or “stressed.” Is it disappointment, embarrassment, resentment, anxiety? Research shows that the emotion can make it easier to observe rather than immediately act on.

Explore what feels threatened. Look underneath the immediate trigger. Are you wanting more certainty, autonomy, respect, belonging, fairness, or recognition?

Then decide what the situation actually requires. Sometimes you need to speak up. Sometimes you need more information to understand the criticism. And sometimes you simply need twenty minutes before replying to that email.

The idea here isn’t to obey every emotion or to eliminate uncomfortable ones, but simply to become better at interpreting them. Name what you feel, explore what feels threatened, and then only choose what the situation actually requires.

Sometimes the emotion will point to something you need to address. Other times, you may realize that the threat exists mostly in your interpretation of what happened. Either way, you’re in a better position to decide what to do next.

Every emotion is a signal. So, instead of judging yourself for having a strong reaction, a more useful question is to ask what exactly it is signaling.

👀 Into the Mind of… #

KATHRYN PAIGE HARDEN

Each week I ask a curious mind about their habits, routines, and rituals. This week we learn from acclaimed psychologist Kathryn Paige Harden, whose book Original Sin explores how genetics complicates our ideas about moral responsibility.

1. One daily practice you can’t do without? Walking on the greenbelt trails near my home. Thinking feels different when my body is moving through physical space. One hour of walking makes all the other hours of my day better.

2. One idea that keeps you up at night? We’ve known for decades that antisocial behavior is just as heritable as depression or body weight. But we’ve devoted few scientific resources to understanding its biology or developing new interventions to change it.

3. One mindset shift that transformed your work? My relationship to praise. Early in my career, I treated positive feedback as a reliable signal that I was producing good work. Now, the reward of work is increasingly the work itself – the opportunity to think deeply about questions I find compelling.
