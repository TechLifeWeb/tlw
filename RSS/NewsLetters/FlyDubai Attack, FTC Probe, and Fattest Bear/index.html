Dubai Flight Attack

A Dubai flight to Tel Aviv plummeted yesterday before passengers broke into the cockpit to stabilize and divert the plane. Israeli authorities say an Omani copilot stabbed his Indian captain midair; Israel is investigating a potential motive.

Roughly two hours after FlyDubai flight 1073 took off, passengers near the front of the aircraft heard an altercation and a cry for help. The plane then nosedived, plunging 14,125 feet in 29 seconds (watch passengers react). A crew member and at least one other passenger broke into the cockpit to subdue the pilot-assailant and regain control of the plane. Another pilot scheduled to operate a later flight executed an emergency landing in Saudi Arabia. Both pilots were hospitalized with stab wounds (see aftermath, warning—sensitive). The plane's roughly 180 passengers later boarded a flight to Israel; a few sustained minor injuries.

FTC Scopes AI

The Federal Trade Commission confirmed yesterday that it is investigating OpenAI, Anthropic, and other leading AI companies over potential dangers to consumers. The probe reportedly predates the July discovery that OpenAI agents hacked into AI platform Hugging Face.

The FTC is also expected to subpoena documents from METR, an AI safety watchdog hired by AI companies to investigate rogue agentic behavior. (Read some of METR’s most notable findings from the Hugging Face hack.) The FTC has not publicly identified the scope of its investigation. The news comes after President Donald Trump hosted the leaders of OpenAI, Anthropic, and other top AI companies Tuesday at the White House. Executives signed an agreement to self-police, emblematic of Trump’s stance that existing regulation is sufficient to protect Americans from rogue agentic behavior.

A new poll yesterday found 73% of Americans are concerned about AI safety. See an overview of various camps in the debate.

Gene-Edited Bananas

Bananas bred to resist browning could expand to more supermarket shelves after passing a major regulatory review last week in the UK. The innovation could reduce food waste, as browning is responsible for roughly 20% of banana yield losses.

Scientists used CRISPR technology (visualize) to alter three genes linked to polyphenol oxidase, an enzyme that causes browning. Unlike traditional genetic modification, this gene editing does not introduce DNA from foreign species and could occur naturally. The change was found to extend the shelf life of peeled bananas by up to 24 hours and make them more resistant to bruising. Gene editing has previously been used to increase vitamin D content in tomatoes and develop sweeter strawberries. The modified bananas have received regulatory approval in 10 other countries, including the US, where they are slated to launch commercially this year.

The Cavendish bananas sold in grocery stores today are the result of over 1,000 years of domestication. The earliest varieties were small with large black seeds (scroll for picture).
