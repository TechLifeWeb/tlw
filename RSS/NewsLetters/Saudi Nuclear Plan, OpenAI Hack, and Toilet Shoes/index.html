US-Saudi Nuclear Deal

President Donald Trump yesterday announced a multibillion-dollar deal to provide Saudi Arabia with a civilian nuclear program—and a potential pathway to enrich its own uranium.

Under the deal, US companies can transfer nuclear technology to Saudi Arabia for up to 30 years. Unlike a 2009 deal with the United Arab Emirates, this deal allows for domestic uranium enrichment if a two-year study deems it warranted; US firms would be tasked with building and overseeing the enrichment facility. If the US declines to proceed with enrichment after the study, the kingdom must wait 10 years before building a facility independently or with another partner. The agreement exempts Saudi Arabia from snap inspections by the United Nations**'** nuclear watchdog, relying instead on direct US oversight.

The deal now goes to Congress, where some lawmakers fear it could open the door for Saudi Arabia to enrich uranium to weapons-grade levels. Blocking it would require a two-thirds supermajority in both chambers.

Contagious Catfish Cancer

Scientists have identified a rare form of skin cancer spreading among catfish, a study published yesterday reveals. The discovery was made in Lake Memphremagog between Vermont and Quebec.

Transmissible cancers are rare and have to date been identified in dogs, clams, shellfish, and Tasmanian devils—bringing the latter to the brink of extinction. In the 2010s, anglers began documenting black spots on the fins and gills of brown bullhead catfish. Researchers identified them as melanoma—a strange diagnosis given the fish's propensity to stay away from the surface and limited UV exposure. Now, scientists studying the tumors’ DNA have discovered they are genetically identical, suggesting they were not caused by UV rays or a viral illness but instead spread among the fish in freshwater. The cancer is so contagious that more than one-quarter of sampled fish have contracted the illness.

AI Goes Rogue

OpenAI revealed its AI models went rogue last week during security tests and hacked into a startup. The security breach is believed to be the first of its kind by an autonomous AI agent.

The incident occurred when OpenAI tested two of its models—GPT‑5.6 Sol and an unreleased model—to see how well they could expose online vulnerabilities. The models were supposed to be confined to a “sandbox,” a closed system where they could not access the internet. However, in an effort to answer the prompt, the models skirted company safeguards and hacked into Hugging Face, an open-source platform for AI technology. The models gained access to internal company systems; Hugging Face said last week it was still investigating whether customer data was compromised. Read OpenAI’s blog post confirming the hack here.

AI agents have become increasingly popular, especially among programmers. But what are the risks? Watch agents create virtual societies, run radio stations, leak documents, and spam people.
