📝 How to Trust What You Know #

Hi friends,

Appearing on the BBC walking barefoot on stage to receive an award was not on my bingo card, but that’s exactly what happened this week!

Last night, Tiny Experiments was the overall winner at the Business Book Awards, a prestigious competition with hundreds of entries each year.

We’re living through a period of enormous change, so it feels special to see a book about experimentation receive this recognition. But the experience also taught me something about preparation and trust, which I’d like to share with you in this edition.

During the awards ceremony, we’d been told there would be no speeches. We were simply supposed to walk on stage, receive the award, smile for the camera and return to our seats. They didn’t mention there was one exception: the overall winner.

So when I was about to walk off stage after smiling for the camera and someone handed me a microphone, I froze for a moment. I spend a lot of time talking about experimentation and self-discovery, but what are you supposed to say when you receive an award?

I started with the obvious answer: saying thank you. I thanked my publisher and my editor, and then I thanked all the authors in the room for continuing to write books at a time when so many of incentives seem to encourage us to make things shorter, faster and easier to consume.

When I returned to my seat, the event publicist came over and told me that they had arranged an interview with the BBC for the following morning to talk about the book. At 5:30am.

Given the hour, I didn’t trust my half-awake brain to produce coherent sentences on live television and decided it’d be sensible to prep a bit, so I wrote down a few answers to questions I thought they might ask.

The interesting thing is that the preparation seemed to make me worse (see the interview on LinkedIn or Instagram).

During the first question, I kept glancing down at my notes, trying to remember the answer I had already decided to give. Watching it back, I can see that I look more stiff and self-conscious than usual.

Then, for the second question, I stopped looking at the notes. I looked at the camera, listened to the question and answered it. Immediately, I looked and sounded much more like myself.

There are some studies that may help explain what happened here. Research on self-focused attention in public speaking suggests that when we become overly conscious of our own performance, part of our attention gets redirected away from the conversation itself and towards monitoring how we’re performing.

Instead of just thinking about the question in front of us, we start running a second cognitive process in parallel: How do I sound? What am I supposed to say next? Am I saying this correctly?

So while my notes were supposed to reduce the risk of freezing, they also created an additional task. I was no longer just answering the question; I was also trying to remember what I had planned to say and checking whether I was saying it correctly.

Once I stopped doing that, the task became much simpler: listen to the question, and answer with what comes naturally to mind.

This doesn't mean preparation is useless, but the aim shouldn’t be to prepare the exact sentences that will come out of your mouth. Instead, what you want is to understand the ideas so well that you can trust yourself to find the words when you need them.

If this is also something you’ve struggled with in the past, here are a few ways you can share your expertise without falling prey to self-focused attention and explicit monitoring:

  1. Prepare ideas rather than scripts. Know the two or three things you genuinely want someone to understand without deciding in advance exactly how you’ll say them.
  2. Put your attention on the other person. Think about the question, the listener and the idea you’re trying to communicate rather than constantly monitoring how you’re coming across.
  3. Trust your brain. If you’ve spent months or years thinking about something, you don’t need to consciously hold all of that knowledge in conscious awareness at once. Trust that it’s already there just waiting for the right question to bring it forward.

The idea is to turn all the work you’ve done into self-trust. You’ve done the reading, the thinking, the practicing beforehand so that, so when the moment arrives you don’t have to control every word that comes out of your mouth.

Instead, you can wonder: Where is this conversation going to take us? What might I discover as I answer? Less control, more curiosity.

On my end, I hope the award helps many more people discover how an experimental mindset can help us navigate uncertainty. And perhaps another lesson here for me is to stop experimenting with new shoes at important events :)
